    Writeup

    Question

    Betting markets are often treated as the best available forecast. A title price only says how likely a team is to win the whole tournament, though, and says nothing directly about the 103 other matches. I wanted to turn those prices into a full match level model, then test it: did the market's view of the field hold up against the real results?

    Method

    • Remove the vig. FanDuel's 48 title prices from June 8, 2026 imply probabilities that sum to 122.9%. I scaled them proportionally to sum to 100%.
    • Goal model. Each team gets one rating. In a match, each side's goals are Poisson with mean 1.3 × e±(gap / 2), so a bigger rating gap means a more lopsided expected score. Draws and goal difference come out naturally, which the group stage tiebreakers need. The three hosts get a small bump in group games.
    • Full 2026 format. Twelve groups of four, the top two plus the eight best third place teams advance, and third place teams are slotted into the Round of 32 using FIFA's allowed group combinations. Knockout draws go to extra time at one third of the scoring rate, then a coin flip for penalties.
    • Calibrate to the market. Starting from a guess, I ran 40,000 simulated tournaments, compared each team's simulated title rate to its market probability, nudged the rating in log space, and repeated 40 times. The fitted ratings reproduce the market's title odds to within a few tenths of a point for every contender. This is the same idea as backing implied volatility out of an option price: find the input that makes the model match the observed price.
    • Score it. For each team and each round (advance, R16, QF, SF, final, title), the forecast probability is compared with what happened. I report the Brier score, log loss, and a Brier skill score against a naive model that gives every team the same chance of reaching each round.

    Results

    • At the market's own spread, the forecast has a Brier skill score of about 0.36, a clear improvement over the naive baseline.
    • The final four were Spain, France, England, and Argentina, all top six in the market. Spain, the favorite at roughly 15%, won it.
    • The surprises came mostly early: Uruguay failing to get out of their group, Germany and the Netherlands both losing Round of 32 shootouts, and Norway beating Brazil.
    • Scaling the rating gaps up improves the score until about 2.4×. On this tournament the market was underconfident in its favorites. That fits the favorite longshot bias, a well documented pattern in betting markets where longshots are overpriced, and here 13 teams sat at the 2,500 to 1 cap.

    Limitations

    • One tournament is one sample. The 2.4× result is suggestive, not a trading rule. Picking the best multiplier after seeing the results is in sample fitting, and checking it properly needs several tournaments.
    • Thin information at the bottom. Title odds can't separate teams that all sit at the sportsbook's maximum price, so those 13 teams share nearly the same rating. Match level odds would fix that.
    • Third place slotting. FIFA uses a fixed lookup table for which third place teams meet which group winners. I use a valid matching under the same allowed combinations, which gives the right opponents in most cases but not every one.
    • Static ratings. The model doesn't update for injuries, suspensions, or form during the tournament.

    What I'd do next

    • Calibrate to closing odds for every group match instead of title odds alone, then compare the two.
    • Run the same pipeline on the 2018 and 2022 World Cups and Euro 2024 to test whether the underconfidence finding holds out of sample.
    • Swap the independent Poisson model for one that accounts for the correlation between the two teams' scores, which matters most for draw rates.
